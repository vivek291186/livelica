There is a particular sound a set of neglected bearings makes. It is not quite a rattle and not quite a hiss, and once you have heard it you cannot unhear it. It is the sound of a skater who spent good money on fast bearings and then let them turn into slow ones. The frustrating part is that bearing maintenance is genuinely easy. It takes twenty minutes, it costs almost nothing, and it is the single highest return piece of skate care there is. Yet most skaters either never do it, or do it badly enough that they would have been better off leaving well alone.

This is a practical guide to cleaning and oiling skate bearings properly. It is not a buying guide, and it is not about which bearing is fastest. It is about the routine that keeps whatever you already own rolling the way it did on day one, and about the specific mistakes that quietly destroy bearings. Whether you run steel or ceramic, inline or quad, the principles are the same.

Why Bearings Slow Down in the First Place

A bearing is a very simple machine. Balls run between two raceways, held in position by a cage, and the whole assembly is protected by seals. When it is clean and correctly lubricated, the balls roll. When it is dirty, the balls start to skid and grind instead, and every one of those tiny frictional events is speed you paid for and did not receive.

Three things cause the decline, and they compound each other.

Contamination

Dust, grit and road film work their way past the seals over time. Once inside, a hard particle sits between a ball and a raceway and does exactly what you would expect a hard particle to do. It scores the surface. That score then collects more debris, and the bearing begins a slow one way trip. This is why the seal design matters so much. Several bearings in the Power Bearings range use double lumen contact seals on polished rims specifically to provide a nearly frictionless barrier against dust, dirt and moisture, which is doing real work every time you skate a dusty rink.

Water

Water is worse than dirt. It flushes lubricant out and it leaves the steel exposed, and steel plus water plus time equals rust. A single skate through a puddle can undo a season of care if you do not deal with it that evening. If you skate somewhere with a monsoon, this is not a theoretical concern.

Wrong or Old Lubricant

This is the one skaters get wrong most often. Lubricant does not last forever. It picks up contamination, it breaks down, and it eventually turns into a paste that is doing more to slow you down than the dirt is. Worse, plenty of skaters reach for whatever is in the garage, which is usually a heavy oil or a general purpose spray, and a heavy oil in a speed bearing is like running in wet sand.

The Cleaning Routine

Here is the process we use. Set aside twenty minutes, work somewhere with decent light, and do not rush the drying step.

Step One: Remove the Bearings

Take the wheels off the frame, then push the bearings out. Most skaters use a bearing puller or the end of an axle as a lever. Whatever you use, do not lever against the inner race with anything sharp, and do not hammer. Keep the bearings in the order they came out if you want to reason about wear patterns later. It is genuinely useful to know that the same position keeps chewing through bearings, because that usually points at a frame or axle problem, not a bearing problem.

Step Two: Deal With the Seals

If your bearings have removable seals, ease them out from the inner edge with a fine pin or a scalpel blade. Go gently. A bent seal never sits right again and a bearing with a compromised seal will fill with grit within weeks. Note that some bearings are built differently. The Power Bearings Swiss Steel Ball, for instance, is designed with a single protective outer seal and an open side precisely so that lubrication is effortless, which is a genuinely thoughtful piece of design if you maintain your kit regularly.

Step Three: Clean

Use a proper solvent in a sealed container and agitate the bearings in it. The goal is to dissolve and carry away the old lubricant along with everything it has trapped. Give them a few minutes, agitate again, and then move them to a second batch of clean solvent for a final rinse. That second rinse is the step people skip, and skipping it means you are leaving a slurry of dissolved grime inside a bearing you just cleaned. If you are not sure what counts as a proper solvent, there is a full section on exactly that below.

Spin each bearing by hand at this stage. Not with compressed air. Spinning a dry, unlubricated bearing at high speed is a reliable way to damage it, and the party trick of blasting a bearing with an air line to make it scream has ruined more bearings than any puddle ever did.

Step Four: Dry Completely

This is the step that decides whether your work was worth doing. Any solvent left inside will thin the fresh oil, and any water left inside will start rusting immediately. Lay the bearings out on a clean cloth and give them time. Warmth helps, aggressive heat does not.

Step Five: Oil

Now the important bit, and the bit where less is genuinely more. One drop per bearing. Not a squirt, not a generous helping. One drop, placed so it can find its way to the raceway, then spin the bearing slowly by hand to distribute it.

Less is more with a speed bearing. One drop finds the raceway and rolls, while a flooded bearing collects dust and drags.

We use Speedslick Pro Bearing Oil, which we developed for exactly this job. It comes in a 10 ml bottle with a fine tip, and it is built around the idea that one drop transforms a bearing into something smooth and fast. A speed bearing does not want to be swimming in lubricant. It wants a thin film in the right place and nothing else. Over oiling is a real mistake, not a harmless one, because excess oil attracts and holds dust and gives you a slower bearing than the one you started with.

Step Six: Reseal and Reassemble

Press the seals back in evenly. They should seat with a soft click and sit flush. Reinstall the bearings, refit the wheels, and check that everything spins freely with no binding when you tighten the axles. If a wheel that spun beautifully on the bench binds up once bolted in, your axle is over tightened or your spacers are wrong.

What to Clean Your Bearings With, and What to Keep Away From Them

Step three asks for a proper solvent and then rather rudely leaves you to work out what that means. It deserves its own section, because the wrong liquid does more damage in ten minutes than a dusty month of skating. A bearing cleaner has two jobs. It has to dissolve old oil and carry grit away, and then it has to leave completely, taking nothing of itself with it and harming nothing on the way out.

Four options meet both tests. A dedicated bearing cleaner is the easy answer because it is formulated for exactly this. A citrus based degreaser works well and is gentle on nylon cages. White spirit, sold as mineral spirit in some places, is the strongest of the four on thick, gummed up old lubricant, and it is cheap, but it rewards patience in the drying step because it is in no hurry to leave. Isopropyl alcohol at 99 percent is the opposite, flashing off in minutes and leaving nothing behind, which makes it ideal for a quick refresh between proper cleans, though it struggles with heavy grease and it will dry rubber seals out if you leave them soaking for hours.

Pick a solvent that lifts old oil and then leaves completely. Anything that attacks the cage or lingers inside costs you more than the dirt did.

The list of things to keep away from your bearings is just as useful. Acetone, which is what most nail polish remover is, is the one that catches skaters out, because it is a superb degreaser and it also attacks the nylon cage and the rubber seals while it works. A cage that has been bathed in acetone can look perfect and then crack under load a fortnight later. Petrol, diesel and kerosene all leave an oily film behind and they bring their own contaminants into the bearing, so you finish with a bearing that is differently dirty rather than clean. Brake cleaner and similar aggressive aerosols strip everything, including the protection on bare steel, and then evaporate so fast that rust can start the same afternoon. Water, washing up liquid and, yes, the dishwasher, are the worst of all, because water reaches every surface inside a bearing and then refuses to leave any of them.

A few practical habits make the whole job cleaner. Work with two sealed jars rather than one, washing in the first and rinsing in the second, and keep them labelled so you never top up the rinse with dirty solvent. Wear gloves, work in moving air, and keep solvent and oil well apart on the bench, because a bottle of oil that has had a splash of solvent in it is no longer the oil you bought. Let the jars stand afterwards so the grit settles, then pour the clean solvent off the top and reuse it, and dispose of the sludge properly rather than down a drain.

Cleaning Bearings With Non Removable Seals

The routine above quietly assumes your bearings come apart, and plenty do not. Before you start prising at anything, look closely at one bearing in good light and work out which of three families it belongs to, because each one wants a different method and the wrong method ruins the bearing.

Three bearings, three methods. Forcing a shield that was never meant to come out does more harm than leaving it in place and flushing instead.

A removable rubber or nylon contact seal is the happiest case. It lifts out from the inner edge with a fine pin, it goes back in with a soft click, and you get the full strip clean described above. A pressed metal shield is the one that needs judgement. Many are held by a thin retaining ring that you can lift out first, after which the shield comes away easily, but some are pressed in and genuinely not meant to be removed. The test is simple. If the shield resists and starts to deform, stop. A bent shield rubs on the balls forever afterwards, and you have traded a dirty bearing for a permanently noisy one.

That leaves the bearings you cannot open, along with open side bearings that need no opening at all. These get a flush rather than a strip. Agitate them in solvent for longer than you would a stripped bearing, change to fresh solvent, agitate again, and then dry them for considerably longer than feels necessary, because the solvent has to find its own way out past the shield. When they are completely dry, put a drop of oil right at the gap between the shield and the inner race and rotate the bearing slowly by hand so the oil is drawn inside. Be honest with yourself about the result. A flush will never reach what a strip clean reaches, so plan to do it more often, and expect to retire those bearings a little sooner. How Often Should You Actually Do This

There is no universal answer, because it depends entirely on where you skate. Some honest guidance:

  • Clean indoor track only, dry conditions: every couple of months is usually plenty.
  • Outdoor road and mixed surfaces: monthly, or whenever the roll noticeably changes.
  • Anything dusty, gritty or humid: more often, and inspect after every hard week.
  • After any contact with water: immediately, that evening, no exceptions.
  • A quick drop of oil between full cleans is fine and takes two minutes.

The better signal than any schedule is your own ear and hand. Spin a wheel. If it sounds gritty or stops sooner than it used to, it is telling you something. Skaters who develop this habit rarely have bearing problems, because they catch them at the stage where a clean fixes it rather than the stage where a replacement is the only option.

Ceramic Bearings Need Care Too

There is a persistent myth that ceramic bearings are maintenance free. They are not. What is true is that the ceramic balls themselves are harder and more resistant than steel, and hybrid ceramic bearings genuinely resist wear better. The Power Bearings ceramic range is built around silicon nitride ceramic balls that are considerably lighter than steel while being tougher, and the Power Bearings Swiss Ceramic is cryogenically treated to refine its molecular structure for efficiency. All of that is real.

But the races in a hybrid bearing are still steel, the cage is still nylon, and the seals still let contamination in eventually. In fact, because a ceramic ball is harder than the steel race it runs on, a contaminated ceramic bearing can damage its own raceways faster than a steel one would. Owning the fastest bearing in the Power Bearings collection and never cleaning it is an expensive way to skate slowly. Clean them on the same schedule you would clean steel.

When to Stop Cleaning and Start Replacing

Maintenance extends life, it does not grant immortality. Retire a bearing when you find any of these:

  • Visible rust or pitting on the races or balls that survives a proper clean.
  • A gritty, notchy feel when you rotate it slowly by hand, even after cleaning and oiling.
  • Any play between the inner and outer race when you try to rock them against each other.
  • A damaged or deformed cage, which will fail suddenly and take the bearing with it.
  • A seal that will not seat properly any more.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use household oil like sewing machine oil or WD40?

Please do not. WD40 is a water displacer, not a lubricant, and it will strip what little oil you have and leave the bearing effectively dry. Household oils are usually far too heavy for a speed bearing. Use something formulated for the job.

How many drops of oil per bearing?

One. Genuinely one. The temptation to add more is strong and it is always wrong.

My bearings sound fine but feel slow. What now?

Clean them anyway. A bearing can be quiet and still be dragging because of thickened old lubricant. Noise is a late symptom, not an early one.

Do I need to clean brand new bearings before use?

Usually no. Bearings sold ready to skate come with appropriate lubricant already in them. Adding your own oil on top of factory lubricant just gives you a heavier mixture.

What if only one bearing in the set is bad?

Replace the pair in that wheel at minimum. A mismatched pair in one wheel puts uneven load on both, and you will be back at the same wheel in a month.

Can I use acetone to clean skate bearings?

No. It cleans brilliantly and it also attacks the nylon cage and any rubber seal while it does so. The damage is invisible at first and shows up later as a cracked cage. Use a dedicated cleaner, a citrus degreaser or white spirit instead.

Is isopropyl alcohol good enough on its own?

For a light refresh, yes, and it has the great advantage of leaving nothing behind. Use it at 99 percent rather than a diluted version, because the water in weaker grades is the one thing you do not want in there. For bearings with thick, gummed up old lubricant it is too weak, so reach for a proper degreaser.

Can I wash bearings in soapy water?

Please do not, and the same goes for the dishwasher. Water finds every internal surface and then leaves none of them, so you are left with wet steel in a sealed space. That is the exact recipe for rust, and the bearing will often feel fine for a week before it starts sounding gritty.

Do I need a bearing cleaning unit?

It is a convenience rather than a necessity. A cleaning unit holds the bearings on a central bolt so you can agitate them all at once in a sealed container, which is tidy and uses less solvent. Two sealed jars and your hands achieve the same result for nothing.

How long should bearings dry before I oil them?

Until there is no smell of solvent and no trace of damp anywhere on them, which usually means hours rather than minutes. Air movement helps far more than heat, so a fan beside an open cloth is better than a radiator or a hair dryer. In a humid climate, give them the whole evening and oil them the same day you clean them.

Why did my bearings rust straight after I cleaned them?

Because a good clean strips the steel bare and leaves it with no protection at all. From that moment the bearing is defenceless until oil goes back in, so any moisture in the air or left inside goes straight to the metal. Clean and oil in the same session rather than leaving stripped bearings on the bench overnight.

Can I dry them with compressed air?

Only gently, and only to blow moisture off the outside surfaces while the bearing is held so it cannot spin. Letting an air line spin a dry bearing is the single most effective way to destroy one, because the balls run far beyond their design speed with nothing between them and the races.

The Habit That Actually Matters

If you take one thing from this, make it the spin test. Every week, pick up a wheel and spin it. Listen. Feel. Compare it to the others. That ten second habit will catch every bearing problem you are ever going to have, at the stage where a clean and a drop of oil fixes it completely. Everything else in this guide is just the procedure you follow once that spin tells you something is wrong.
