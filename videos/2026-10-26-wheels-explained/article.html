Bearings get all the conversation. Walk into any rink or track and you will hear skaters comparing ceramics, arguing about ABEC ratings and swapping opinions on oil. Meanwhile the four things actually touching the ground, the only parts of your setup that convert your effort into forward motion, are usually chosen because they were the ones in stock.

Wheels are the single biggest performance variable on an inline speed skate. They decide how much grip you get in a corner, how much rolling resistance you fight on the straight, how the skate feels over rough tarmac, and how many kilometres you get before you are shopping again. Get them right and everything else in your setup works better. Get them wrong and the best bearings in the world will not save you.

This is a full guide to choosing wheels: what the numbers mean, what actually changes when you move between them, and how the JUNK and MPC ranges we stock compare in practice.

Wheel size: what 90mm, 100mm and 110mm really change

Wheel diameter is the first decision, and it is more consequential than most skaters realise. Bigger is not simply better.

The case for large wheels

A larger wheel covers more ground per revolution. It also rolls over surface imperfections more easily, because the wheel meets a bump at a shallower angle. On a smooth track or a good road, a 110mm wheel carries speed beautifully and holds it with less effort once you are up to pace.

The costs are real though. Large wheels raise your ride height, which raises your centre of gravity and makes the skate feel less stable and less immediately responsive. They are heavier, so they take more work to accelerate and more work to swing through each stride. And they demand a frame that actually accommodates them.

A larger wheel meets the same bump at a shallower angle, so it rolls over rough tarmac more easily, but the axle sits higher and the skate feels less planted.

The case for smaller wheels

A 90mm or 100mm wheel accelerates faster, sits lower, and gives you a more connected, more controllable feel. On a tight indoor track with constant corners and constant acceleration out of them, this matters enormously. Many skaters who chase 110mm because it is what the top marathon skaters use end up slower on a technical track than they would be on 100mm.

Choosing honestly

Ask what you actually skate, not what you aspire to skate. Long, smooth, open road and marathon distances point to 110mm. Tight indoor tracks, technical banked circuits and lots of accelerating out of corners point to 100mm or 90mm. Rough road surfaces, where you are dealing with real world tarmac rather than a swept track, tend to reward the larger diameter simply because it survives the bumps better.

Your frame decides the ceiling. Check the maximum diameter your frame is rated for before you buy anything. This is not negotiable and it catches people out constantly.

Durometer: the firmness question

Durometer measures how hard the urethane is, on the A scale. The trade off it controls is the oldest one in skating: grip versus roll.

The basic physics

Softer urethane deforms more where it meets the ground. That deformation is grip, and it is also energy lost as heat. Firmer urethane deforms less, so it rolls with lower resistance and lasts longer, but it gives you less mechanical grip and transmits more vibration to your feet.

There is no universally correct answer. There is only the correct answer for your surface, your weight and your skating.

XFirm versus XXFirm in practice

Most of the race wheels we stock come in two firmness options. For the JUNK range, XFirm is rated 84 to 86A and XXFirm is rated 86 to 88A. Those two points of overlap are why skaters describe the difference as a shade rather than a step: XFirm holds a little more, XXFirm rolls a little further.

Choose XFirm when you want more grip, when the surface is slick or dusty, when the track is cool, when you are a lighter skater who does not load the wheel much, or when you are still developing your corner technique and want the wheel to help you rather than punish you.

Choose XXFirm when the surface has good natural grip, when the temperature is high (heat softens urethane, so a warm day effectively makes every wheel softer than its rating suggests), when you are a heavier or more powerful skater who can overwhelm a softer wheel, or when you want maximum roll and maximum life and you have the technique to manage the reduced grip.

XFirm and XXFirm overlap at 86A, so the difference is a shade of feel rather than a jump. Hot tarmac effectively softens whatever you fit.

Temperature matters more than the internet admits

This deserves its own mention for Indian conditions. Urethane firmness is temperature sensitive. A wheel that feels perfect in an early morning winter session can feel greasy and vague on hot afternoon tarmac in May or June. Skaters who race across seasons often keep two sets and swap them, and that is not overkill, it is just paying attention.

Rotation: the free speed most skaters ignore

Inside edges wear faster; heel and toe wheels wear differently. Coned wheels give inconsistent grip. Swap positions and flip so the worn side faces out, every couple of weeks for serious training. Use rotation to inspect urethane for chunking, hubs for cracks, bearings for grit; clean and re oil.

Getting more life out of a set

Rotate on schedule, keep them clean, store cool and out of sun, match compound to surface, skate clean technique.

Frequently asked questions

110mm only if your frame and skating suit it. XFirm is the safer first set. Do not improvise mixed sizes; check the frame. A sharp lip on the inside edge means rotate. Judge life by wear, not months.

The short version

Pick diameter by surface and skating you do. Pick durometer by surface, weight and temperature. Rotate religiously. Keep bearings clean.
