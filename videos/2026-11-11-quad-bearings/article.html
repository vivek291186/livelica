Why quad skaters have to check first

Almost all inline speed skates use the 608 bearing, and because inline dominates the speed skating conversation, 608 has become the default assumption everywhere. Quad skating sits outside that default. A great many quad setups, including quad speed and quad long distance skates, are built around a 627 axle instead. That single difference means a large share of the bearings marketed to speed skaters simply will not fit a quad skate.

None of this is a problem once you know about it. It only becomes a problem when a skater assumes that a bearing is a bearing. Check your axle size once, write it down, and you will never order the wrong set again.

The one number that matters, the bore

The bore is the hole in the middle of the bearing, the part that slides onto the axle. That is where 627 and 608 differ. A 627 bearing measures 7mm bore by 22mm outer diameter by 7mm wide. The common 608 differs by having an 8mm bore. That one millimetre is the whole story. A 627 will not slide onto an 8mm axle, and a 608 will rattle loosely on a 7mm one.

Because the outside of the two bearings is so similar, you cannot reliably tell them apart by eye across a table. The names give it away, and so does the axle, which is why measuring or checking your frame specification is the only dependable method.

Only the bore changes. That single millimetre decides whether the bearing fits your axle at all.

How to check which size you are on

This takes about two minutes and saves you a wasted order. Any one of these will give you the answer, and it is worth doing two of them if you want to be certain.

  • Pull an existing bearing out of a wheel and read the shield. The size is usually printed on it, so you will see 627 or 608 stamped there.
  • Measure the axle itself. A vernier calliper is ideal, but a decent steel rule will do. 7mm means you need 627 and 8mm means you need 608.
  • Look up the specification for your plate or frame from the manufacturer. Most list the axle size directly.
  • Ask before you order if you are still unsure. A quick message costs nothing and a wrong set of sixteen bearings costs a lot.

One more thing worth knowing. 627 is not exclusively a quad size. Some inline frames are also built around a 627 axle, so if you skate inline and your bearings sit loose or refuse to seat, run the same check rather than assuming 608.

Never force a bearing onto an axle to test the fit. If it does not slide on with light pressure, it is the wrong size, and forcing it damages both the bearing and the axle.

How many bearings a quad setup needs

Once you have the right size, the next question is quantity, and quad skaters sometimes order half of what they need. Every wheel takes two bearings, one on each side, so the wheel spins evenly on the axle. A quad skate carries four wheels, which means eight bearings per skate. A pair of skates therefore needs sixteen bearings in total.

Two bearings per wheel, four wheels per skate, two skates. Sixteen is the number to order.

What ABEC actually tells you

ABEC ratings cause a lot of confusion, so it is worth being clear. ABEC measures manufacturing tolerance, so a higher number means the bearing was made to tighter tolerances. What it does not do is describe everything that decides how a bearing feels underfoot. Cage design, seal quality, ball material and how well the bearing is maintained all matter alongside the rating. That is why the 627 ABEC 11 lists its P6 precision, nylon cage and sealing rather than leaning on the ABEC number alone. Treat ABEC as one useful piece of information, not the whole verdict.

Fitting and looking after them

Once you have the right size and quantity, the rest is straightforward. Seat two bearings into each wheel, one from each side, with a spacer between them if your wheels use one. Slide the wheel onto the axle and tighten so the wheel spins freely without side to side play. Overtightening is the most common setup error and will slow you down more than any rating will speed you up.

Maintenance keeps a good bearing good. Sealed bearings resist dust and water well, but no seal is permanent, and grit that gets past will grind away your roll. Clean them when they start sounding dry or feeling slow, and re oil them with a light, clean oil such as Speedslick Pro Bearing Oil. Ceramic handles heat and water better than steel, but it still needs clean oil to run at its best.

Frequently asked questions

How do I check whether my skates use 627 or 608?

Look at the axle diameter or your frame and plate specification. A 627 axle is 7mm and a 608 axle is 8mm. If you have an old bearing to hand, the size is usually printed on it. If you are still unsure, message us before ordering.

Can I make a 608 bearing fit a 627 axle?

No. A 608 has an 8mm bore and will sit loose on a 7mm axle, which is unsafe and will damage the bearing. Always match the bearing to the axle rather than trying to adapt it.

Are 627 bearings only for quad skates?

No. They are most common on quads, but some inline frames run 627 axles too. The axle size decides the bearing, not whether the skate is quad or inline.

How many bearings do I need for my quads?

Sixteen for a standard pair of four wheel skates, since each wheel takes two bearings and each skate has four wheels.

Do ceramic bearings need oiling?

Yes. Ceramic copes with heat and water better than steel, but it still needs clean, light oil to run at its best. Do not assume ceramic means maintenance free.

Is a higher ABEC number always faster?

Not on its own. ABEC describes manufacturing tolerance, not real world speed. Seals, cage design, ball material, lubrication and how clean you keep the bearing all affect how fast you actually roll.
