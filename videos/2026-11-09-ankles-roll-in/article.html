If your ankles roll in on inline speed skates, the cause is usually not weak ankles. It is almost always one of four things: your weight sitting inside the wheel line, wheels worn into a slope, a frame mounted off to one side of where your foot loads, or a boot that is not holding your heel down. Work through those four before you start any strength work, because three of them change the same afternoon you fix them.

This happens to nearly every skater in their first season, and it comes back for experienced skaters who change wheel size or move to a new frame. One line of wheels gives you a single narrow strip of contact with the road, so your foot has nowhere to cheat. Whatever error sits above the skate, the ankle is where it gets settled. The useful question is which error.

Rolling in is not the same as leaning: what the wobble actually is

Every stroke in speed skating puts the skate on an edge on purpose. You set the wheel line over, load it, and push away from it. That is not collapse. Collapse is when the skate tips inward while you are simply gliding, with no push being made, and you cannot stop it happening.

The signs worth looking for:

  • the skate tips inward during the glide, not only during the push
  • one skate's wheels are visibly more coned than the other's
  • your arch aches after a session while your quads feel barely used
  • standing still in skates, your kneecap sits inside the line of your big toe
  • you make constant small corrections with the ankle instead of steering from the hip

If none of that sounds familiar and the skate only tips when you are pushing hard, you are probably fine. Edge work is the sport.

Start with the two minute standing test

Before you buy anything or start any exercises, do this on a flat floor at home. It takes two minutes and it tells you whether the problem lives in your gear or in your foot. Work through the steps in order.

  • Put the skates on and do the laces exactly as you normally would. Stand next to a wall.
  • Hold the wall lightly, look straight down the front of your shin, and note where your kneecap sits compared with the middle of your foot.
  • Let go of the wall and stand on one skate for a few seconds. Then the other. Watch the frame, not your knee.
  • Loosen the laces from the ankle upward and repeat. If the roll improves with a looser cuff, the closure is forcing it.
  • Step out of the skates and do the same single leg stand barefoot.

Read it like this. If the ankle rolls in barefoot too, your foot and ankle are part of the story and the strength section below applies. If your barefoot stance is quiet and the roll only appears once the skates are on, the gear is doing it to you, and no amount of calf raises will fix that.

Collapse is not a lean. In a lean the whole column tips together. Here the shin is still upright while the boot and frame fold in underneath it, which leaves the load hanging to the inside of the only strip of road you are standing on.

Cause one: your weight is inside the wheel line

This is the most common cause by a long way, and it is a technique problem rather than a gear problem. A single file of wheels gives you a contact strip only a few millimetres across, and your foot has to be parked on top of it. When the hips sit even slightly to the inside of that strip, the ankle is left holding a sideways load it has almost no leverage against, so it tires quickly and starts to fold.

The giveaway is timing. The roll is worst at low speed and during long glides, and it almost disappears when you are skating hard and changing feet quickly. Our guide to single leg balance for inline speed skating has the full progression for getting your mass over the wheels, so we will not repeat it here. The short version is that the correction belongs to the hip, not the ankle, and it is built by holding low glides on one skate for far longer than feels useful.

Cause two: wheels worn into a slope

This one is quietly responsible for a lot of mysterious ankle trouble, and it is easy to miss because the wheels look fine from above. Speed wheels do not wear flat. They wear from the shoulder you load, and for most skaters that is the inner shoulder on both skates, because the push drives the wheel onto its inside edge stroke after stroke. Track skaters get a second pattern layered on top of that one, because cornering the same way every lap adds wear to the left side of every wheel, which is the inner shoulder on the right skate and the outer shoulder on the left. That is why a track set often ends up less symmetrical than a road set. Either way, as a shoulder goes, the crown of the wheel migrates off centre. The wheel stops presenting a high point in the middle and starts presenting a ramp, so your skate sits at a small angle every time you load it.

Every skater cones wheels to some degree, so coning on its own is not proof that you roll in. What matters is how far it has gone and whether the two skates match. Take a wheel off, hold it at eye level, and look at the profile end on. If the two shoulders are not mirror images of each other, that wheel is tilting your foot, and if one skate is noticeably worse than the other you have found a reason why one ankle feels weaker than its partner.

Two things help. Rotate your wheels on a schedule rather than when you happen to remember, and when you do it, flip each wheel over and move it to the other skate as well as to a new position. Moving wheels front to back on the same skate leaves the same side loaded, so only flipping and swapping actually evens out a profile. The second thing is to accept that a set which has coned badly is finished for training that needs stability, even if there is urethane left on it. Our guide to reading wheel wear and coning covers how to judge where that line sits, and our JUNK and MPC wheels collection shows what the compounds and sizes look like in 90mm, 100mm and 110mm when you come to replace a set. Two things are worth knowing before you do. Replacing all eight at once gives you eight matched profiles, which does more for stability than any single new wheel ever will. And until you replace them, demote the coned set to easy sessions and skate your rounder set on the days stability matters.

Cause three: frame position and ride height

The wheel line wants to sit directly underneath the point your foot actually loads. When it does not, the load hangs off to one side of the contact patch and the skate tips that way for the whole session, with no technique fault involved at all. A skate that tips inward is telling you the load is falling inside the wheel line, which means the frame is sitting further out than your foot wants it.

The orange dot is where your foot presses down. When the wheel line sits outboard of it, every bit of load you put through the skate is also a small turning force rolling the boot inward.

Most speed boots allow a few millimetres of lateral adjustment at the mounting points, so this is usually fixable without buying anything. Mark the current position on the boot sole with a pencil before you loosen the bolts, then move the frame in toward the inside of the foot in steps of about two millimetres, one skate at a time so you have something to compare against. Stop and go back a step if the arch starts complaining, because a frame shifted too far inside loads the arch heavily on every stroke and will trade one problem for another. Our guide to mounting inline speed frames covers the bolt work in detail. It is quick and it costs nothing, so do it alongside the wheel check rather than after it.

Ride height is the other half of this. A taller wheel lifts you further off the road, so the same small tilt at the ankle carries your weight further out from the wheel line. The lever you are fighting gets longer, which means the same ankle angle produces a bigger toppling force and a small error turns into a big one faster. If you are fighting stability on 110mm wheels, dropping to a 90mm or 100mm setup for a block of training is a legitimate move rather than a step backward. Cause four: the boot is not holding your heel

Worth saying plainly: there is nothing for us to sell you in this section. LiveLica does not carry inline boots or insoles. What we build is what bolts underneath.

If your heel lifts inside the boot, your foot slides a few millimetres on every stroke and lands somewhere slightly different each time. The ankle spends the whole session hunting for a position it never gets to keep, and from the outside that looks exactly like a weak ankle.

The lacing instinct here is wrong. Most skaters pull the top of the cuff as tight as they can, which restricts the forward ankle flex you need for a low position and leaves the heel just as free as it was. Pull the laces hard over the instep, where the eyelets sit above the arch, because that is the band that drives the heel back into the heel cup. Above the ankle bone, ease off. A cuff you can still fold forward with your hand is a cuff that will let you get low. If the roll changed when you loosened the cuff in the standing test, this is your fault to fix. Sizing and heat moulding matter as well, and our guide to choosing, sizing and fitting inline speed boots goes through both properly.

Ankle and foot work that actually helps

If the barefoot test showed a roll, strength work belongs in your week. Be realistic about the timeline though. Gear faults change the same afternoon you fix them. Strength changes over six to eight weeks of consistent, boring work. That is why it comes fourth on this list and not first.

Ten minutes on most days is plenty. The balance progression lives in the single leg balance guide, so what follows is the ankle specific work that sits alongside it rather than a second copy of the same list.

  • Resisted inversion. Anchor a band on the outside of your foot and turn the sole inward against it. This loads tibialis posterior, the muscle that holds the arch up and resists the foot rolling in, and almost nobody trains it.
  • Short foot holds. Draw the ball of the foot gently back toward the heel so the arch domes, keeping the toes flat and relaxed, and hold ten seconds. Done barefoot on a hard floor, this teaches the arch to hold a shape instead of being propped up by an insole.
  • Tibialis anterior raises for the front of the shin, which balance out all the calf work speed skating already gives you.
  • Slow calf raises through the full range, two legs first and then one leg once that is easy, three sets, controlled on the way down.

None of this needs equipment and none of it needs to be hard. Consistency is the whole trick.

When to get it looked at

Everything above is general training guidance, not a diagnosis. See a physiotherapist or a doctor rather than working through it yourself if you have sharp pain in or around the ankle, visible swelling, numbness or tingling in the foot, an ankle that gives way under you, or any pain that lasts more than a few days after you stop skating. An ankle that has been sprained before deserves professional advice on loading it, because the ligaments and the balance reflexes both need specific work after an injury and a generic list will not cover it.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need skates with more ankle support?

A taller, stiffer cuff does make a wobbling ankle feel safer, and for a first season that trade can be worth it. The cost is range of motion, because you cannot push at a deep angle through a cuff that will not let the ankle move. Decide it on the standing test: if the roll vanishes when you tighten the lower boot, you need fit, not a taller cuff. If it is still there with the boot locked down, more cuff will buy you a season while you work through the four causes above.

Do insoles help?

Sometimes. A supportive insole can stop the arch collapsing inside the boot, which removes one part of the chain. It will not correct a frame sitting off to one side or a wheel that has worn to a ramp, so do the standing test first and find out what you are actually treating. If the pain is the main complaint rather than the wobble, our guide to sore feet in inline speed skates works through the pain patterns instead.

How to stop your ankles rolling in on inline speed skates: where to start

Do the two minute standing test tonight. If the roll disappears barefoot, check your wheel profiles and your frame position before you look at yourself. If it is there barefoot as well, start the ankle work and give it two months, while keeping an eye on the red flags above and getting it looked at if any of them show up. Either way, hold more single leg glides than feels comfortable, because a skater who can stand quietly on one skate at low speed has already solved most of this.
